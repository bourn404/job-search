# Writing in Carson's Voice — Style Guide

How to write cover letters (and other prose) that read like Carson wrote them. Built from his own letters
in `examples/` and from what he kept, cut, and rewrote across the Sept/Oct 2026 letters (Beast Industries,
Reddit, Stripe, Airbnb, Apple, Salesforce, Amazon Leo, GM, Ramp, Roblox). When this guide and an older
letter disagree, this guide wins. Last updated 2026-11-03.

## Hard rules (checked before every render)

- **No em dashes (—).** Use commas, parentheses, or separate sentences.
- **No colons in prose.** The setup-colon-reveal sentence ("X has one thing in common: Y") reads as
  AI-written. Split it into two sentences or join with a comma.
- **No direct quotes**, from the JD, the company's site, its blog, or its values page. Paraphrase plainly.
- **Don't attribute statements back to the company.** Not "Roblox has said most traffic begins on Home" or
  "Ramp's growth team says…"; write "Home is where most players start." It reads like quoting them to
  themselves.
- **No AI-trope metaphors or filler:** "north star", "tapestry", "navigate", "at the intersection of",
  "synergy", "passionate about", "results-driven", "leverage cutting-edge". (He uses "leverage" as a noun
  about scope, sparingly.)
- **Banned templated phrases:** "I am writing to express my interest", "I am excited to apply", "proven
  track record", "detail-oriented", "team player", "fast-paced environment", "hit the ground running",
  "I believe I would be a great fit", "leverage my skills", "thank you for considering my application".
- **No repeated phrasing inside a letter.** "I'd love to help" (or any distinctive phrase) appears once.
- **Contractions, first person, warm and direct.** Write "and", not "&", in prose.
- **Sign-off, by default:** `Thank you for your time & consideration,` then `Carson Fairbourn`. A
  company-flavored sign-off he picks replaces it (see Greeting and sign-off).
- **Length:** 3 to 4 short paragraphs, about 200 to 300 words, one page. His approved letters land around
  210 to 250 once he has cut what he doesn't need.

## Greeting and sign-off

A named person only when known with confidence (never guess). Otherwise **default to `Dear <Company>
Team,`**. At a huge company, a one-word team is fine ("Dear Search Team," "Dear Monetization Team,"), but
never the full department name: he shortened "Dear GM Design System Team," "Dear Search Design System
Team," "Dear Monetization Engineering Team," and "Dear Thumbtack Design Systems Team," to the company or a
one-word team (2026-10). A company-flavored greeting from their own vocabulary is welcome when short:
"Dear Builders of the Impossible," (Beast), "Dear Space Makers," (Calendly), "To the people who make
Stripe beautiful,", "Dear Ramp Builders,". Never "Dear Hiring Team," or "To whom it may concern."

**Offer one flavored pair.** He has asked for a less generic greeting and closing several times (ClickUp,
Calendly) and kept company-flavored sign-offs: "Are you still watching?" (Netflix), "May you always find
what you're searching for," (Google), "TL;DR: thank you for your time & consideration," (Reddit). In the
opener round, also offer **one** company-flavored greeting and **one** sign-off next to the defaults. In
auto mode, use the defaults unless an earlier letter to the same company has an approved flavored pair.

## The opener: conviction first, humor optional

He wants the first two lines to sell him on their own, and he usually picks a **sincere, conviction-led
opener** over a joke. Across Airbnb, Amazon Leo, GM, Ramp, and Roblox he passed on every punchline option.
What he chose:
- A belief about the work: "The best marketing platforms get engineers out of the way." / "A good design
  system makes the right choice the easiest choice." / "A marketing site moves at the speed its marketers
  can test ideas."
- A belief about the company's space: "Most finance software is tolerated." / "Signing in, proving who you
  are, and paying are the moments a platform asks for the most trust…"
- A real personal story: "Some of my earliest memories of belonging are from the gaming forums of the
  late 2000s…" (Roblox).
- His own motivation: "The work I'm proudest of has helped people who could easily have been left out."

Humor still works when it's mild and his: the Apple letter's "look at how Apple does it… possibly enough
to count as a catchphrase." When offering openers, give **3 to 4 conviction-led options drawn from his
signature ideas, plus at most one light-humor option**. Never finalize an opener he hasn't picked. If he
says none land, offer new directions rather than variations of the same ones.

When there is humor: craft-adjacent, warm, never snarky; one line, then straight to substance; the
sentence after the laugh is plain and sincere (that's the line he keeps).

## Structure

1. **Opener** (above): a conviction or real motivation, 1 to 3 sentences.
2. **One story, told in depth.** One accomplishment with its specifics and why it mattered, not a list.
   Lead with the outcome he cares about (quality, enablement, trust, belonging), then what he did, then a
   number or two that belong to that story. He likes showing **systems thinking**: closing issues wasn't
   enough, so he moved the work upstream (design system, enforced conventions, review agents).
3. **Why this company**, plainly. Tie his real work to what the company exists to do or how it builds. One
   real reference, stated as a plain fact, and **only from safe sources**: the JD, the company's careers or
   mission page, or its values page. Never product release notes or "recent updates" (Discord: "we could be
   wrong and making assumptions"), never internal project, system, or design-system names (Stripe's Sail,
   Netflix's UI Paved Road), never old or obscure blog posts or talks, and no claims about what changed
   recently.
4. **Close (optional)**: end on the why-this-company paragraph unless there's a short callback to the
   opener ("left out" → "lets them in"; "tolerated" → "the part of their day they actually like"). He cut
   standalone cute closes (Calendly's "I'll send you my Calendly link", Pinterest's "Win or learn, I'm
   looking forward to finding out"). Don't overstate what the role will accomplish.

**One point per paragraph.** Don't end a paragraph by bolting on a second credential (accessibility, scale,
tooling) that belongs to a different story. He cut every one of these: "And since I run SchoolAI's
accessibility program, I'd make sure it works for everyone" (Calendly), "It also happens at real scale,
where the shared components…" (Capital One), "I also built the tooling that makes flags…" (Pinterest).
Don't carry the letter's theme into more than two paragraphs ("beating a dead horse", GM).

A second letter to the **same company** may reuse the earlier letter's story when it still applies to the
new role (Carson, 2026-11-03); tell a new story only when the old one doesn't fit.

## Voice and wording

- **Plain and sincere beats polished.** Say it the way he'd say it out loud ("I want my work to count for
  something"). Avoid constructions like "X comes backed by Y".
- **Credit precisely.** Match the bullet's credit level: "led" only where he led (onboarding, the 6-team
  accessibility initiative, the microinteraction initiative), "helped" or "the team" where it was shared
  (activation result: "the team raised…" / "I helped raise…"). He corrected "delivered our first VPAT" to
  "led to delivery of our first VPAT, which unblocked deals".
- **Keep incidental specifics out.** Don't state team sizes ("two-person design-system team") or echo the
  JD's internal details back ("greenfield", "small senior team", "from the ground up"). They read as
  over-specific or technical; say what the work is and why it matters.
- **Name the practice, not a tool list.** "Our conventions are enforced in the tooling engineers and agents
  both run into," not "GritQL, Biome, and ESLint rules" (tool lists read as oversimplifying quality).
- **Mission over role-overreach.** Align with what the company says it does; don't claim the job will
  change the world.
- **Address the company broadly**, not the reader's own job ("the way MrBeast tests thumbnails," not "the
  way your team tests…").
- **Values over name-drops.** No specific employees or their talks.
- **Rhythm:** vary sentence openings (no "I built… I led… I shipped…" parade); split sentences much past
  ~30 words unless he wrote them long on purpose.
- **His lines are the base.** When he writes or rewrites a line, polish lightly and say what changed.

## Signature ideas (use where they fit; they make strong openers)

- The **pit of success**: a platform or design system makes the right thing the easy, default thing.
- **By default, not an afterthought**: accessibility and quality as defaults.
- **The conditions for quality, not just the output.**
- **Accessibility outcomes are downstream of design-system work.**
- **Find out you're wrong, fast**: hypothesis, ship the variant, read the numbers, repeat (growth work).
- **AI as leverage**, with him still accountable for every line it writes.
- **Belonging**: late-2000s gaming forums (bungie.net group, game nights); people finding their community.

## Tone

Confident but humble; principled without preaching; specific and earnest. A craftsperson explaining why
the work matters to him, not a candidate selling himself. Vocabulary leans: craft, quality, by default,
downstream, conditions, ownership, the right thing, enablement.

## Reference letters (reuse what he approved)

Every approved spec in `specs/` holds a final letter in `coverLetter`, and `specs/feedback/<spec>.json`
(local) shows what Carson changed and why: the before/after edits teach his taste better than the finals.
Read 2 or 3 that fit the role. **For a similar role at a different company, reuse his approved paragraphs**
(the story, the premise) and adapt them lightly; he refined those lines on purpose and wants the same things
highlighted (2026-10-06). Write fresh only the company-specific parts (why this company, its fact, a
callback close). The same goes for a second letter to the **same** company when its story still fits. Where an older
letter breaks a rule above (colons and quotes were allowed before 2026-09-30), the rule wins.

## Before rendering: checklist

- [ ] No em dashes, colons, or quotation marks in the body; no "[Company] says/has said".
- [ ] Opener is one he picked; the first two lines sell him on their own.
- [ ] One story in depth, with credit wording that matches what he actually did.
- [ ] One plain fact about this company from a safe source (JD, careers or mission page, values page); no
      release notes, internal names, or "recently" claims.
- [ ] The story matches the role type (table above).
- [ ] Each paragraph makes one point; no bolted-on last sentence; theme in at most two paragraphs.
- [ ] No banned phrases or AI-trope metaphors; no phrase repeated across paragraphs.
- [ ] About 200 to 300 words, 3 to 4 paragraphs, `Dear <Company> Team,` (or a picked flavored greeting).
- [ ] `render` printed no lint warnings (the mechanical rules above are checked in code).
